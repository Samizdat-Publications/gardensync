# Companion planting research log

The table in `src/domain/plants/companions.json`: 53 pairs across the 63 library plants. 22 are
"keep apart" pairs and 31 are "put together" pairs; 46 are marked `tested` and 7 `traditional`.

## Method

- **Start sceptical.** Most companion charts copy each other. A pair is `tested` only when a trial,
  an extension publication or a well-established mechanism backs it, and every `tested` pair cites one
  source that I opened during this pass and that returned HTTP 200 (checked with curl at the end).
- **Mechanism first.** The pairs that hold up fall into a few groups: shared diseases and pests
  (late blight, Verticillium wilt, Colorado potato beetle, corn earworm); trap crops and insectary
  plants with field results (collards, arugula, Blue Hubbard squash, sweet alyssum, nasturtium);
  conflicting soil or water needs (Mediterranean herbs against thirsty crops, blueberry pH); spreading
  roots (mint); and space and timing (radish as a row marker, quick greens before slow or late crops).
- **Traditional pairs** were kept only when gardeners will look for them (tomato and basil, the three
  sisters, carrots and onions, asparagus and tomato, borage and strawberry). Each reason says plainly
  that it is traditional and what, if anything, was found when it was tested.
- **Reasons claim no more than the source.** Single-study results say so ("the evidence rests on that
  one study"). Trap crops say what they cost (the trap is sacrificed, or must be treated).
- **Small beds.** A "keep apart" soil-borne disease pair (Verticillium) is rotation advice in the
  source: the problem is shared soil. In a planner that places plants in the same bed, shared soil is
  exactly what a neighbour means, so these are marked `bad`.
- One entry per unordered pair, `a` and `b` in alphabetical order, generated by a script that checks
  ids, duplicates, reason length (all under 210 characters) and em dashes before writing the file.
  `npx vitest run tests/unit/plant-library.test.ts` passes (255 tests).

## 7.x pairings dropped and why

- **Cucumber and melon kept apart "to stop cross-pollination".** They are different species
  (Cucumis sativus, Cucumis melo) and do not cross; and crossing within a species changes only the
  seed, never this year's fruit.
- **Beet and Swiss chard as enemies.** The same species, Beta vulgaris. They cross only when both
  flower, which matters to seed savers, not to a bed of greens.
- **Potato against cucumber, zucchini, pumpkin, melon, watermelon and winter squash.** Usually
  justified as "shared blight". Cucurbits do not get potato late blight, and no evidence was found
  for the rest.
- **Potato and sunflower; sunflower as a general enemy.** Sunflower allelopathy is shown mainly with
  extracts in the lab. Extension answers found no garden evidence and say garden cultivars are much
  weaker than wild sunflowers. Too weak to flag, so it is left out entirely.
- **Onion, garlic and chives against beans and peas.** Widely repeated, but no trial evidence was
  found. Dropped rather than kept as lore, because it would steer people away from a harmless mix.
- **Tomato against cabbage, broccoli and kale; brassicas against strawberry; pepper against
  kohlrabi; spinach and chard against potato.** No mechanism and no evidence found.
- **Dill against carrot and tomato.** Dill and carrot cross only for seed savers; the tomato claim is
  unsupported.
- **Marigold as a companion for everything** (potato, pepper, beans, cucumbers, eggplant). UF/IFAS
  says interplanted marigolds probably do not reduce nematodes (they work as a dense stand grown
  first), and UMN says several studies contradict marigold against Colorado potato beetle and flea
  beetles. Kept only for tomato (whitefly trial) and cabbage (one study, worded as such).
- **"Peas fix nitrogen for the greens"** and similar legume pairings. Legumes fix nitrogen mostly for
  themselves; neighbours gain little in the same season. No nitrogen-for-neighbour pair was kept.
- **Carrot and tomato** ("carrots love tomatoes"). A book title, not a finding.
- **Rosemary, sage, mint and thyme repelling cabbage pests broadly.** Only the single Iowa result for
  thyme on broccoli is kept, worded as one study.
- **Sage and rosemary "stunting" cucumber; mint "harming" parsley.** Kept as `bad` but for the real
  reasons: water needs and mint's spread.
- **Blueberry against tomato, pepper and eggplant.** Lore; replaced by the real conflict, soil pH.
- **Nasturtium with tomato, cucumber, radish and beans; borage with tomato, cucumber and zucchini;
  calendula, sunflower and sweet alyssum with many crops.** No crop-specific evidence found. Alyssum
  in broccoli gave mixed results (USDA ARS), so only alyssum with lettuce is kept.
- **Basil against sage and rue; cilantro against dill; corn against tomato as "competition".** No
  evidence (corn and tomato are kept apart for the earworm instead).
- **Broken references.** 7.x pointed at ids that never existed or are not in the 8.0 library
  (fennel, rue, catnip, sweet peas, hollyhock, cantaloupe, squash, green beans, corn, chive and
  others). Everything here is checked against the 8.0 ids.

## Uncertain

- **Verticillium pairs** come from rotation advice. Treating "same bed" as "same soil" is my
  inference, and the effect builds over years rather than in one season.
- **Corn earworm** moving from corn to tomatoes is documented; that planting them together raises
  damage is plausible but was not trialled in the sources.
- **The Iowa brassica results** (thyme, onion, nasturtium, marigold) rest on one study reported by
  UMN Extension; I could not open the original report (the Iowa State repository blocked the fetch).
- **Blue Hubbard** protects other squash only as a full border that is treated or hand-picked; in a
  small bed the benefit is unproven.
- **Carrot and onion** has some trial support but is marked `traditional`, because the effect was
  small and only held while onions were young and outnumbered the carrots.
